How to Install a Video Doorbell: Wiring, Transformer Power, and Chime Setup

Oct 15, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
A video doorbell is one of the most satisfying smart-home upgrades you can make: an afternoon of work puts a camera, motion alerts, and two-way talk on your front step. But unlike most smart gadgets, a wired video doorbell needs continuous power from your home's low-voltage doorbell circuit, and getting the transformer and chime wiring right is what separates a reliable install from one that drops offline every night. This guide walks through the whole job, from checking your existing wiring to fixing the failures that catch most beginners out.

How a wired doorbell circuit actually works

Your doorbell runs on a simple loop. A small transformer, usually tucked next to your breaker panel or in a closet, steps household 120-volt AC power down to a gentle 16 to 24 volts AC. From the transformer, two thin bell wires run out to the button at your front door and to the chime box inside the house, forming one continuous loop. Pressing the button completes the loop, current flows, and the chime sounds. Release the button and the circuit goes quiet again.

A video doorbell changes the deal. The old button was a dumb switch that only drew power for the second you pressed it. A video doorbell is a small computer with a camera, Wi-Fi radio, microphone, and speaker that must stay powered around the clock. It sips a small current continuously through the same two wires, which is why the transformer's capacity suddenly matters. Most video doorbells want 16 to 24 volts AC with a rating of 20 to 40 volt-amps (VA). The 16-volt, 10 VA transformers installed in millions of older homes were sized for a momentary buzz, not a 24/7 camera, and they are the number-one cause of doorbells that work by day and die at night.

One reassuring fact: the doorbell wires themselves carry low-voltage AC, so the two conductors at your front door are safe to handle and polarity does not matter. You cannot wire them backwards. The dangerous part of this project is not at the door at all; it is at the transformer, where one side connects to full mains voltage. Keep that distinction clear and the whole job stays firmly in beginner territory.

Before you buy: audit your existing doorbell

Start with a five-minute survey. Do you have a working wired doorbell now? Look for the button at the door, the chime box (usually a small grille-covered box in a hallway), and the transformer itself: a small metal block about the size of a matchbox, often mounted on or near the breaker panel, in a basement, garage, or closet. If the house never had a doorbell, skip ahead to the section on homes with no existing wire; the rest of this guide assumes those wires exist.

Next, measure what the transformer is actually delivering. Set a multimeter to AC volts, pull the button off the door (the power can stay on for this; it is low voltage), and touch the probes to the two wires. A healthy circuit reads between 16 and 24 volts. Below 16, or a reading that sags badly, means the transformer is weak or overloaded. Then read the fine print on the transformer itself: you want a VA rating of at least 20, ideally 30 to 40. A 16V/10VA unit, extremely common in older homes, is almost certainly inadequate for a video doorbell and should be upgraded before you mount anything.

Two more pre-purchase checks. First, stand at your front door with your phone and check Wi-Fi strength; a doorbell with one bar of signal will give you one bar of reliability. If the signal is weak, plan to move a mesh node or access point closer before installation day. Second, identify your chime type: a mechanical chime has visible metal bars or plungers that physically strike, while a digital chime plays an electronic melody from a speaker. Your video doorbell's app will ask which one you have, and digital chimes often need the small power adapter from the kit wired in to behave. Knowing all four answers, voltage, VA rating, Wi-Fi, and chime type, before you buy turns installation day into a calm hour instead of a troubleshooting marathon.

Tools, materials, and the safety rules

The toolkit is short: a Phillips screwdriver, a drill with bits and wall anchors, wire strippers, a multimeter, and a non-contact voltage tester. Your phone does double duty as the Wi-Fi survey tool and the setup screen. On the materials side you need the video doorbell kit itself (bracket, screws, and usually a chime power adapter included), a few small wire nuts or lever connectors, and, if your audit found a weak transformer, a replacement rated 16V/30VA or 24V/40VA.

Safety first, with one crucial distinction. The bell wires at the door and chime are low voltage and safe to handle. The transformer's primary side is wired directly to 120-volt mains, and that is where respect is required. Before touching the transformer or opening any box it is mounted in, switch off the breaker feeding it and verify with your non-contact tester. Swapping a transformer means making mains connections (black to black, white to white, ground to ground) inside or beside the panel area, and the panel's main lugs stay live even with the breakers off. If that sentence makes you nervous, that is good judgement talking: have a licensed electrician swap the transformer while you handle the easy low-voltage doorbell end yourself. It is a short, inexpensive service call.

Step by step: mounting and wiring the doorbell

With the breaker off and the wires at the door verified dead (your multimeter should read zero volts AC), remove the old button. Take a photo of how the two wires connect, then free them. If you are upgrading the transformer, do it now while the power is off: mount the new unit where the old one was, connect the mains side exactly like-for-like, land the two bell wires on its screw terminals, and close the box up before restoring power.

Mount the new bracket at about 48 inches (1.2 metres) from the ground, centred on the door frame or the wall beside it. Hold a level against it, mark your holes, drill, and set the anchors. Angle matters: a doorbell aimed at the sky films clouds, and one tucked behind a pillar films the pillar. Most kits include a small wedge to angle the camera toward the walkway; use it if your door sits at an angle to the path visitors take.

Now the wiring, which is the easiest part of the whole project. Connect the two bell wires to the two terminals on the back of the doorbell; with AC, there is no positive or negative, so either wire can go to either terminal. Tighten the screws firmly and give each wire a gentle tug. If your kit includes a chime power adapter, wire it at the chime box according to the manual: it typically connects across the chime's Front and Trans terminals and gives the doorbell a constant trickle of power while still letting the chime ring normally. Do not skip this little module; it is what prevents the humming and buzzing problems covered in the troubleshooting section.

Clip the doorbell onto the bracket, restore power at the breaker, and give it a minute to boot. Open the manufacturer's app, add the device, connect it to your Wi-Fi (choose the 2.4 GHz network if offered; it reaches the front door far better than 5 GHz), and set the chime type to match what you identified earlier: mechanical, digital, or none. Then test everything like a visitor would: press the button and listen for the chime, check that your phone gets the alert, and open the live view in daylight and after dark. A clean install passes all four tests on the first try.

No existing doorbell wire? Your options, ranked

Plenty of homes never had a wired doorbell, and you still have good options. The simplest is a battery-powered video doorbell from the same product families: it mounts with two screws, needs no wiring at all, and just asks for a recharge every few months. The trade-off is maintenance, and cold weather noticeably shortens battery life, so in harsh climates expect winter recharges.

The middle path is a plug-in transformer. These small adapters plug into an indoor outlet and output the same 16 to 24 volts AC your doorbell wants; you run a pair of thin bell wires from the adapter to the door, often tucked along trim or through a small drilled hole. No mains wiring, no electrician, and the doorbell gets continuous power exactly like a hardwired install.

The full retrofit is running new bell wire through the walls: fish 18-gauge two-conductor bell wire from a transformer location to the door and to a chime spot. The low-voltage fishing itself is very doable DIY (the same skills as running thermostat wire), but the transformer's mains connection belongs to an electrician. And if you do not want a wired chime at all, most video doorbells can pair with a wireless plug-in chime that you simply plug into any indoor outlet. Pick the option that matches your appetite for drilling, and remember that even the simplest battery install benefits from the Wi-Fi check in section two.

Troubleshooting: the failures that catch everyone

The classic symptom is a doorbell that works fine all day and goes offline every night. Night is when the infrared LEDs switch on and power draw peaks; a marginal transformer that copes by day sags below what the doorbell needs after dark. If your doorbell reboots or drops Wi-Fi at dusk, measure the voltage at the door while the camera is in night mode. A reading dipping under 16 volts confirms it: upgrade to a 24V/40VA (or at least 16V/30VA) transformer and the nightly dropouts stop.

A chime that hums or buzzes constantly is the second classic. The doorbell draws a tiny continuous current to stay alive, and on a mechanical chime that trickle can be just enough to make the plunger vibrate without striking. The fix is the chime power adapter from the kit, wired across the chime terminals as the manual shows; it gives that trickle current a proper path. If the buzzing started after you changed a setting, check the app's chime type: telling the app you have a digital chime when you actually have a mechanical one (or vice versa) produces exactly this symptom.

If the chime does not ring at all when the button is pressed, work backwards: confirm the app's chime setting matches your hardware, check that the chime wires landed on the correct Front and Trans terminals (a photo from disassembly pays off here), and verify the chime itself still works by briefly touching its two doorbell wires together with the power on. No ring from that direct test means the chime mechanism has failed, not your wiring.

Finally, know when to stop and call a licensed electrician: if you cannot identify which breaker feeds the transformer, if the transformer is buried inside the main panel rather than mounted beside it, if you smell burning or see scorch marks anywhere in the circuit, or if the wiring looks nothing like this guide describes. A video doorbell install is a genuinely beginner-friendly project right up to the edge of the mains wiring, and recognising that edge is what keeps it safe.
