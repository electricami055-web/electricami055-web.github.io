How to Solder Wires Together: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide

Nov 6, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
To solder two wires together, strip 10-15 mm of insulation from each end, twist the strands tight, tin both ends with a thin coat of solder, join them with a lap or Western Union splice, and heat the wires — not the solder — with an iron set to 315-350°C (600-662°F) for leaded solder or 350-400°C (662-752°F) for lead-free, until the solder flows into the joint in two to three seconds. Slide heat-shrink tubing over the wire before you start, let the joint cool completely still, then shrink the tubing over it for insulation. The whole job takes under five minutes once your iron is hot, and a good joint is shiny, smooth, and stronger than the wire itself. This guide walks through the exact steps, the three splices worth knowing, and the mistakes that make weak, dull joints.

What you need: tools and materials for soldering wires

The kit for wire soldering is small, and every item earns its place. You need a soldering iron or station of at least 40-60 watts with temperature control, a roll of rosin-core solder (0.8 mm diameter is the most versatile size for wire work), wire strippers matched to your gauge, a pair of helping hands or a small clamp, heat-shrink tubing, and a heat source for the tubing — a hot-air gun, a heat gun, or a lighter used carefully. A 60-watt temperature-controlled station is the sweet spot: it holds 320°C steady while a cheap 30-watt pencil iron sags the moment it touches a thick wire, which is the most common reason beginners get cold joints.

Pick the solder to match the job. Classic 63/37 tin-lead solder melts at 183°C (361°F) and is the easiest to learn with: it flows smoothly, wets copper well, and cools to a bright, shiny joint. Lead-free SAC305 (96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper) melts higher at 217°C (423°F) and needs a hotter iron, but it is the right choice if you solder often or sell what you build, since many regions restrict lead in consumer products. Either way, use rosin-core solder wire: the flux inside the core cleans the copper as it melts, and separate liquid flux is only needed for old, oxidized wire. Keep a roll of 0.8 mm solder on the bench; thicker 1.0-1.2 mm solder dumps too much metal onto small joints, and thin 0.5 mm solder is slow on anything above 18 AWG.

Heat-shrink tubing is not optional — it is the insulation. Buy a 2:1 polyolefin assortment kit covering 1-14 mm diameters, which handles everything from 22 AWG signal wire to 12 AWG power wire, and always slide a piece onto one wire before you solder, because you cannot add it after the joint is made. A 3:1 adhesive-lined tube is worth keeping for outdoor or automotive joints: the glue melts as it shrinks and seals the joint against water. The other consumable people forget is tip care — brass wool to wipe the tip between joints and a small blob of fresh solder to re-tin it — because a clean, shiny tip transfers heat in seconds while a black, oxidized tip cannot, whatever the dial says.

The 7-step method: soldering two wires together

Step 1: slide the heat shrink on first. Cut a piece of tubing about 25-30 mm long, slightly wider than the finished joint, and slide it onto one of the wires, well away from the joint area. Forgetting this step is the single most common beginner error, and there is no fix except cutting the joint apart and starting over.

Step 2: strip the wires. Use wire strippers to remove 10-15 mm (about 1/2 inch) of insulation from each end, choosing the stripper notch that matches the gauge — a notch too small nicks strands and weakens the wire, a notch too large leaves insulation behind. If the wire is stranded, twist the bare strands tightly between your fingers so they form one neat bundle with no stray whiskers sticking out. Stray strands are a short-circuit waiting to happen, especially inside a crowded enclosure.

Step 3: tin both ends. Heat your iron to the working temperature — 320°C (608°F) for leaded solder, 370°C (698°F) for lead-free — and let it stabilize for 30-60 seconds. Hold the tinned tip against one stripped end for two to three seconds, then touch the solder wire to the hot copper, not to the iron. The solder should melt and wick into the strands, coating them with a thin, shiny layer. Repeat on the second wire. A properly tinned end looks silver and smooth; if it looks dull or blobby, wipe the tip, add a touch of fresh solder, and try again.

Step 4: join the wires. For a quick repair, use a lap splice: lay the two tinned ends alongside each other so they overlap by the full stripped length and hold them steady in helping hands. For a stronger joint, use a Western Union splice: cross the bare ends at right angles, then wrap each end tightly around the other wire three to four turns. The mechanical wrap carries the load, so the solder only has to make the electrical connection — this is the splice NASA's workmanship standards call for on wires that will see tension or vibration.

Step 5: heat the joint and feed the solder. Touch the iron tip to the underside of the joined wires and hold it there for two to three seconds until the wires themselves are hot enough to melt solder. Then touch the solder wire to the top of the joint, away from the iron. Good solder flows toward the heat and wicks between the strands; a good joint takes only a small amount — enough to fill the gaps, with the wire strands still faintly visible through a smooth, concave fillet. If the solder balls up instead of flowing, the wires are not hot enough yet: keep the iron in place and wait, rather than adding more solder.

Step 6: let it cool completely still. Remove the iron and the solder, then hold the joint motionless for five to ten seconds while it solidifies. Moving the wires while the solder is pasty fractures the bond and creates a dull, grainy cold joint that can fail intermittently — the hardest kind of fault to find later. Do not blow on the joint; rapid cooling can cause the same grainy structure.

Step 7: insulate with heat shrink. Slide the tubing over the joint so it covers the bare metal plus 5 mm of insulation on each side, then shrink it with a heat gun or the hot-air nozzle of a rework station, sweeping the heat from the center outward. Polyolefin tubing shrinks at roughly 90-120°C, so a few seconds of hot air is enough; a lighter works in a pinch, but keep the flame moving or the tubing scorches. When the tube is tight against the wire with no air gaps, the joint is finished. Give it a firm tug test — a good soldered wire joint is stronger than the wire itself, and the wire should break before the joint does.

Three wire splices compared: which joint for which job

Not every soldered joint is built the same way, and the splice you choose decides how much pulling, vibration, and bending the connection can survive. The lap splice is the fastest: two tinned ends laid side by side and soldered, with the overlap carrying the load. It is fine for bench repairs, prototype wiring, and joints that will be supported or potted, but it relies entirely on the solder for strength, so it is the wrong choice for anything that will be tugged or flexed repeatedly.

The Western Union splice is the standard for wires under tension. The bare ends cross and wrap around each other three to four turns, so the copper itself locks the joint together before any solder is applied, and the solder then seals and protects it. It takes a minute longer and needs a little practice to wrap neatly, but the result survives pulls that would tear a lap splice apart, which is why it shows up in antenna work, outdoor lighting runs, and repairs inside walls where you cannot come back to fix a failure.

The lineman's splice is the heavyweight: the strands are intermeshed, then each side is twisted tightly around the other wire in a long, symmetrical wrap. It is the strongest of the three and the standard for long overhead pulls and field repairs, but it is overkill for a quick bench fix and fiddly on fine stranded wire below 22 AWG. For most electronics and hobby work, the rule is simple: lap splice for a supported joint you can reach again, Western Union for anything that moves, vibrates, or runs outdoors.

One splice belongs nowhere on this table as a finished connection: twisting wires together and wrapping them in electrical tape. It corrodes, loosens, and overheats, and no amount of tape makes it a reliable joint. If you cannot solder, use a proper crimp butt connector or a solder-seal connector with a heat gun instead — both are legitimate, testable connections, while twist-and-tape is a fire risk dressed up as a repair.

Soldering different wires: stranded vs solid, thin vs thick

Stranded and solid wire behave differently under the iron, and the technique changes with them. Stranded wire — the flexible kind in appliances, automotive harnesses, and electronics — solders easily because the flux and solder wick between the strands, but it must be twisted tight first, and any stray strand left sticking out is a short-circuit risk. Solid wire — the stiff copper in house wiring and breadboard jumpers — holds its shape well and takes a Western Union wrap cleanly, but it sinks more heat, so give the iron an extra second or two on the joint and use a chisel tip of 2-3 mm that can deliver heat across the full wire diameter.

Gauge drives the iron setting more than anything else. Thin 22-24 AWG signal wire (0.5-0.6 mm diameter) solders at the bottom of the range, around 300-320°C for leaded solder, and overheats fast — two seconds is plenty, and longer contact melts the insulation back from the joint. Medium 18-20 AWG wire, the common size for DC projects and LED strips, is comfortable at 320-350°C. Thick 12-14 AWG power wire (2.0-2.6 mm diameter) is a heat sink: move to the top of the range, 350°C for leaded and up to 400°C for lead-free, use a broad chisel tip, pre-tin both ends first, and expect the joint to take four to five seconds to flow. If a thick joint still will not flow, the fix is a bigger tip or a higher-wattage iron, not a longer dwell — holding a 350°C iron on a wire for fifteen seconds cooks the insulation and anneals the copper.

Mixed metals need care too. Soldering copper to tinned brass terminals or to a connector pin works the same way, but the pin is a bigger heat sink than the wire, so heat the pin first and let the wire join it. Never try to solder aluminum wire with electronics solder and flux: aluminum oxidizes instantly, the solder will not wet it, and the joint will fail. Aluminum wiring needs listed aluminum-rated connectors and anti-oxidant compound, and in a house that is a job for a licensed electrician. The same caution applies to joining wires of very different gauges — a 12 AWG wire to a 24 AWG wire — where the thin wire overheats long before the thick one is ready; pre-tin the thick wire heavily, then make the joint quickly with the iron mostly on the thick side.

Five mistakes that make weak, dull joints

Cold joints are the number one failure, and they are easy to spot: dull, grainy, or blobby instead of smooth and shiny. They happen when the wires never got hot enough for the solder to bond — usually because the iron was touched to the solder instead of the wires, the tip was oxidized, or the joint was moved while cooling. The fix is always the same: clean and tin the tip, heat the wires until they melt the solder themselves, and hold the joint perfectly still for five to ten seconds. If a joint looks dull, reflow it once with fresh solder and a clean tip rather than piling more solder on top.

Too much solder is the second mistake, and it looks like a round blob swallowing the joint. A good joint uses just enough solder to fill the gaps between strands, with the wire shape still visible under a smooth, concave fillet. Excess solder adds weight, hides bad wetting underneath, and can bridge to a neighboring wire. Too little is just as bad — a starved joint shows bare copper and cracks under vibration. The right amount is learned by feel in a dozen practice joints on scrap wire, which is why every beginner should practice on offcuts before touching a real project.

The third mistake is forgetting the heat shrink, covered above, and the fourth is soldering with a dirty tip. A black, oxidized tip cannot transfer heat, so the iron reads 350°C while the joint sees far less, and the natural response — turning the dial up to 420°C — only oxidizes the tip faster. Break the cycle by wiping the tip on brass wool between joints and re-tinning it with fresh solder before parking the iron. The fifth mistake is skipping flux on old wire. Wire that has sat in a drawer for years has an oxide layer that rosin-core solder alone may not cut through; a dab of liquid rosin flux on the stripped ends before tinning makes the solder flow like new wire. Never use acid-core or plumbing flux on electronics — it corrodes the copper over weeks and destroys the joint from the inside.

When to solder — and when to crimp instead

Soldering is the right call for electronics, prototypes, and low-voltage DC wiring: sensor leads, LED strips, battery packs, Arduino projects, and repairs to appliance cords. The joint is electrically excellent, mechanically strong when spliced properly, and sealed against corrosion once heat shrink goes over it. For anything that stays put and carries under a few amps, a soldered joint with heat shrink is the most reliable connection a hobbyist can make.

Crimping wins where vibration, flexing, or high current are involved. In cars, boats, and motorcycles, a proper ratcheting crimp with adhesive-lined heat shrink outlasts solder, because solder wicks up the strands and creates a stiff section right where the wire flexes — that stiff-to-flexible transition is where vibration cracks the copper. The same logic applies to wires that get plugged and unplugged often. A quality crimp tool and the right terminal are not a compromise; for automotive and marine work they are the professional standard, and solder-seal butt connectors — a heat-shrink tube with a ring of low-temperature solder inside — give you a soldered connection and a crimp-style strain relief in one step, shrunk with a heat gun in about thirty seconds.

House wiring is a different world with its own rules. Never solder mains wiring as the connection itself: electrical code requires splices in walls to be mechanically secure before any solder is applied, and in practice that means listed wire nuts or lever connectors inside an accessible junction box — not a soldered joint buried in a wall. Soldering a 120V or 240V conductor and wrapping it in tape is not a repair, it is a fire hazard, and if you are unsure whether a wire is mains voltage, turn off the breaker, verify with a non-contact tester, and call a licensed electrician. The soldering in this guide is for low-voltage electronics and appliance cords, where the voltages are 24V DC or less and a mistake costs a project, not a house.

Safety runs through all of it. A 350°C iron tip causes a serious burn on contact, so always return the iron to its stand and never leave it powered unattended. Soldering fumes come mainly from the flux, not the metal, so work in a ventilated area or with a small fume extractor. If you use leaded solder, wash your hands after handling it and before eating, and keep food and drinks off the bench. Lead-free solder removes the lead concern but still produces flux fumes and still burns at 370°C, so the ventilation and hand-washing habits stay the same either way.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you solder two wires together without a soldering iron?

Use solder-seal butt connectors: slide one over each wire end, crimp lightly to hold them, then heat the connector with a heat gun for about 30 seconds until the solder ring inside melts and the tubing shrinks tight. For a purely mechanical option, a ratcheting crimp butt connector with adhesive-lined heat shrink works with no heat source beyond the crimp tool. Both give a testable, insulated joint without an iron.

What temperature should I solder wires at?

For leaded 63/37 solder, set the iron to 315-350°C (600-662°F), with about 320°C as a good default for most wire gauges. For lead-free SAC305 solder, use 350-400°C (662-752°F), with about 370°C as a good default. Thin 22-24 AWG wire works at the bottom of the range, while thick 12-14 AWG wire needs the top of the range and a broad chisel tip.

Should you twist wires together before soldering?

Yes. For stranded wire, twist the bare strands of each end tightly first so no whiskers stick out, then join the ends with a lap splice or a Western Union splice — crossing the ends and wrapping each one around the other three to four turns. The mechanical wrap carries the load so the solder only has to make the electrical connection, which makes the joint far stronger than solder alone.

How do you solder thick wires together?

Pre-tin both ends first, use a 60-watt or higher temperature-controlled iron with a broad 2-3 mm chisel tip, and set it to the top of the range — 350°C for leaded solder, up to 400°C for lead-free. Heat the wires for four to five seconds until they melt the solder themselves, and use a Western Union splice so the copper carries the mechanical load. If the joint still will not flow, the tip is too small or the iron is underpowered, not the temperature too low.

Is soldering wires better than using wire nuts or crimp connectors?

It depends on the job. Soldering is best for electronics and low-voltage DC wiring, where it makes an electrically excellent, sealed joint. Crimping is better for automotive and marine wiring, where vibration cracks soldered joints at the stiff-to-flexible transition. For house mains wiring, neither is a DIY choice — use listed wire nuts or lever connectors inside a junction box, and call a licensed electrician if you are unsure.
