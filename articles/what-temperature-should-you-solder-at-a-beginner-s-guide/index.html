What Temperature Should You Solder At? A Beginner's Guide

Nov 4, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
For most electronics soldering, set your iron to 315-350°C (600-662°F) for leaded solder and 350-400°C (662-752°F) for lead-free solder. Those two ranges cover nearly every hobbyist joint, from small resistors on a circuit board to thicker wires on a switch or connector. The single most common beginner mistake is not the temperature number itself but guessing at it: a fixed-wattage iron with no temperature control drifts hot or cold depending on the joint, while a temperature-controlled station holds the setting you choose. This guide explains the right temperature for each solder type, why the iron setting sits far above the solder's melting point, and how to adjust for big joints, delicate parts, and lead-free wire.

The short answer: temperature by solder type

Start with the alloy on your spool, because that decides the range. Classic tin-lead solder, usually 63/37 or 60/40 tin-to-lead, melts at about 183°C (361°F), and the standard working setting for hand soldering is 315-350°C (600-662°F). Most experienced hobbyists leave a leaded-solder station parked around 320°C (608°F) and only move it for unusually large or small joints. Lead-free solder, most commonly SAC305 (96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper), melts higher at about 217°C (423°F), so its working range is 350-400°C (662-752°F), with 370°C (698°F) a good default. The roughly 30-50°C gap between the two alloys is the single most important number in this guide: if you switch from leaded to lead-free wire and keep the old setting, the solder will feel sluggish, dull, and reluctant to wet the joint.

If you only remember one rule, make it this: use the lowest temperature that still lets you form a shiny, smooth joint in two to three seconds. A joint made quickly at the right temperature beats a joint baked for ten seconds at a low one, because long contact time is what actually overheats components and lifts copper pads. When in doubt between two settings, pick the lower one first and raise it in 10-20°C steps until the solder flows freely and wets both surfaces.

Why the iron setting is far hotter than the melting point

Beginners often ask why the iron is set to 320°C when the solder melts at 183°C. The answer is thermal mass and speed. The tip must heat the component lead and the copper pad, not just the solder wire, and both act as heat sinks that pull heat away the moment the tip touches them. Setting the iron roughly 100-150°C above the alloy's melting point gives enough thermal headroom that the joint reaches melting temperature in a second or two instead of five or ten. That short contact time is what protects the component: a resistor or capacitor can survive a two-second touch at 320°C far better than a ten-second soak at 260°C, because damage comes from total heat delivered over time, not just the peak number on the display.

Tip size matters as much as the temperature number. A fine conical tip stores little heat, so it cools quickly on a large joint even at a high setting; a chisel tip of 2-3 mm holds more heat and transfers it faster, which is why it is the default for general electronics work. If your solder refuses to melt at 350°C, the problem is usually a dirty or oxidized tip, an undersized tip for the joint, or a low-wattage iron that cannot recover temperature fast enough — not a need to crank the dial to 450°C. Clean and tin the tip first, switch to a chisel tip, and only then raise the temperature.

Wattage and temperature are different things, and confusing them causes real mistakes. Wattage describes how fast the iron can replace lost heat; temperature describes the target it holds. A 60-watt temperature-controlled station at 320°C will solder a ground-plane joint that a 30-watt fixed iron at an unmeasured 400°C+ struggles with, because the station recovers its temperature between joints while the fixed iron sags. For electronics work, a 60-75 watt temperature-controlled station is the sweet spot, and it is what makes the temperature ranges in this guide repeatable rather than guesswork.

Adjusting for the job: delicate parts, big wires, and ground planes

The ranges above are starting points, and real boards need small adjustments. For delicate work — small surface-mount parts, thin sensor leads, and heat-sensitive components such as LEDs and plastic connectors — drop toward the bottom of the range, around 300-320°C (572-608°F) for leaded solder, and work fast with a fine tip. The goal is a joint made in under two seconds. If the solder will not flow at that setting, do not hold the iron on the part longer; instead, clean the tip, add a touch of fresh flux-core solder to improve heat transfer, and try again.

At the other extreme, large joints need more heat, not more time. Ground planes, thick battery wires, switch terminals, and connector shells all sink heat rapidly, so move to the top of the range — 350°C for leaded, up to 400°C for lead-free — and use a broad chisel tip that can deliver heat quickly. Pre-tinning heavy wire ends before joining them also helps, because the solder already coats the strands and the final joint needs less heat to complete. A useful test: if the joint takes more than three to four seconds to flow, your tip is too small, your temperature is too low, or the tip is oxidized. Fix one of those three things rather than leaning on the joint until the board discolors.

Two special cases deserve their own settings. Soldering to a metal chassis or a large copper pour is essentially soldering to a heat sink; use the top of the range, a large tip, and, for lead-free work, up to 400°C. Hot-air rework for surface-mount parts is a different process entirely, with air temperatures typically in the 300-380°C range and airflow doing the work — do not confuse the hot-air dial with the iron dial on a combination station. And for stained glass or heavy-gauge wire work outside electronics, irons run hotter still, often 400°C and up, but that is a different craft with different solder and flux.

What goes wrong at the wrong temperature

Too cold is the more common beginner failure, and it is easy to recognize. A cold joint looks dull, grainy, or blobby instead of smooth and shiny, and it may crack or fail intermittently because the solder never truly bonded to the metal — it just froze around it. Cold joints also tempt beginners into the worst habit of all: wiggling the joint while it cools, which fractures the bond as it solidifies. The fix is a clean tip, enough heat to flow in two to three seconds, and holding the joint perfectly still until the solder sets. If a joint looks dull, reflow it once with fresh solder and a clean tip rather than adding more solder on top.

Too hot has its own signature damage. Above about 450°C (842°F), flux burns off instantly, so the solder stops wetting and balls up instead of flowing; tips oxidize within minutes, turning black and refusing to take solder; and copper pads can lift off the board, which is often unrepairable on a cheap PCB. Excess heat also damages components directly — melted plastic connectors, cracked ceramic capacitors, and LEDs that dim or die. The rule of thumb used by repair technicians is that 480°C is the ceiling for electronics irons, and most work should never need more than 400°C. If you find yourself above 400°C for ordinary joints, something else is wrong: a dirty tip, the wrong tip size, or an iron without enough wattage.

Tip care is really temperature care. An oxidized tip cannot transfer heat, so the iron reads 350°C while the joint sees far less, and the natural response — turning the dial higher — only oxidizes the tip faster. Break the cycle by tinning the tip with fresh solder before first use, wiping it on brass wool (not a wet sponge, which shocks the tip) between joints, and re-tinning before parking the iron in its stand. A tip that stays shiny solders well at 320°C; a blackened tip solders poorly at 420°C. Replacing a $2-5 tip beats replacing a lifted-pad board every time.

Setting and holding the right temperature on your station

On a digital station, the process takes under a minute. Identify your solder alloy from the spool label — 63/37 and 60/40 are leaded, SAC305 and Sn99Cu0.7 are lead-free — and set the starting point from the table above: 320°C for leaded, 370°C for lead-free. Let the iron fully reach temperature (most stations show a stable readout within 30-60 seconds), tin the tip with a small amount of fresh solder, and make a test joint on scrap wire or an old board. A good joint flows in two to three seconds and cools to a smooth, concave, shiny fillet. If it balls up or looks dull, adjust in 10-20°C steps and retest rather than jumping 50°C at a time.

On a fixed-wattage iron with no display, you cannot set a temperature at all — the tip temperature depends on the wattage, the tip size, and how much heat each joint draws. That is acceptable for occasional wire splices, but it is the reason temperature questions matter so much for electronics: without a controlled station, every joint is a guess. If you solder more than a few times a month, a 60-watt digital station with a 200-480°C range and °C/°F display is the single upgrade that improves joint quality most, more than any exotic solder or tip. Sleep mode, which drops the temperature when the iron sits idle, is worth having too: it roughly doubles tip life by slowing oxidation between joints.

Safety belongs in any temperature guide, because the numbers here are genuinely hot. A 350°C tip causes a serious burn on contact, so always return the iron to its stand, never leave it powered unattended, and keep the cord routed so it cannot drag the iron off the bench. Soldering fumes come mainly from the flux, not the metal: work in a ventilated area or with a small fume extractor, and if you use leaded solder, wash your hands after handling it and before eating. Keep a damp cloth and the habit of tinning the tip, and the temperature that makes good joints is the same temperature that stays safely under control.

Frequently Asked Questions

What temperature should I set my soldering iron to for electronics?

For leaded solder (63/37 or 60/40), set your iron to 315-350°C (600-662°F), with about 320°C as a good default. For lead-free solder such as SAC305, use 350-400°C (662-752°F), with about 370°C as a good default. Use the lowest temperature in the range that still forms a shiny joint in two to three seconds, and adjust in 10-20°C steps from there.

Can I solder at 250°C?

Usually not for electronics joints. At 250°C the joint barely exceeds the melting point of lead-free solder (217°C) and leaves little headroom to heat the pad and lead, so the solder flows sluggishly and often forms a dull, weak cold joint. The exception is very small, delicate work with leaded solder and a well-tinned tip, where some technicians work around 280-300°C — but 250°C is too cold for reliable general soldering.

Why won't my solder melt even at 350°C?

The most common cause is an oxidized or dirty tip, which insulates the joint from the iron's heat — clean it with brass wool and re-tin it with fresh solder. The next suspects are a tip too small for the joint and an iron without enough wattage to recover temperature on a large copper area. Raising the dial past 400°C rarely fixes these problems and usually just oxidizes the tip faster.

What temperature is too hot for soldering electronics?

Above about 450°C (842°F) is too hot for ordinary electronics work: flux burns off before it can clean the joint, tips oxidize within minutes, and copper pads can lift off the board. Most electronics joints should never need more than 400°C, and if you are reaching for higher settings, the real problem is usually a dirty tip, the wrong tip size, or an underpowered iron rather than a need for more heat.

Do I need a temperature-controlled soldering station, or will a fixed-wattage iron do?

A fixed-wattage iron works for occasional wire splices, but its tip temperature drifts with every joint, so you can never reliably hit the ranges in this guide. A 60-watt digital station with a 200-480°C range holds the temperature you set, recovers between joints, and shows the actual reading — which is why it is the single upgrade that most improves joint quality for anyone who solders more than a few times a month.
