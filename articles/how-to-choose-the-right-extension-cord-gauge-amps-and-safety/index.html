How to Choose the Right Extension Cord: Gauge, Amps, and Safety

Oct 08, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
Nearly every home has a drawer full of extension cords, and nearly every home uses at least one of them wrong. A thin indoor cord powering a space heater, two cords chained together to reach the lawn mower, a cable buried under a rug — these ordinary habits are behind thousands of electrical fires every year. The good news is that choosing the right cord takes about two minutes once you know what the numbers mean. This guide explains gauge, amperage, and length in plain language, shows you how to read the printing on a cord jacket, and lays out the safety rules that genuinely matter.

Why cord size matters more than you think

An extension cord is a length of wire, and wire resists the flow of electricity. Thinner wire resists more. When current pushes through that resistance, two bad things happen: energy is lost as heat in the cord itself, and the voltage arriving at your tool drops below what the tool was designed for.

Heat is the fire risk. A thin cord carrying a heavy load warms up along its whole length. Under a rug or coiled in a bundle where heat cannot escape, that warmth becomes a genuine overheating hazard, softening insulation and, in the worst cases, igniting nearby material. This is why the size of the conductors inside the cord is a safety question, not just a performance one.

Voltage drop is the performance problem. Motors are especially sensitive to it: a circular saw fed 105 volts instead of 120 runs hotter, produces less torque, and can stall under load. Repeated stalling and overheating shortens motor life dramatically. Electronics with switch-mode power supplies usually cope better, but they still draw more current to compensate, which pushes more heat back into the cord. In short, an undersized cord punishes both ends of the connection.

Step one: find your tool's amperage

Every sizing decision starts with one number: how many amps your device draws. The honest place to find it is the nameplate, the sticker or stamped plate on the tool itself, which lists amps directly. A typical corded drill draws 5 to 7 amps, a circular saw 12 to 15, a space heater around 12.5, and a shop vacuum anywhere from 8 to 12.

If the nameplate only lists watts, divide by 120 to estimate amps: a 1,500-watt heater draws about 12.5 amps. This works well for resistive loads like heaters and incandescent work lights. For motors, treat the watts-based estimate as a minimum and prefer the marked amperage, because motors draw extra current at startup and under load, and the nameplate figure already accounts for real-world behaviour.

If you plan to run two devices on one cord through a multi-outlet end, add their amperages together. This is where people get caught out: the drill alone was fine on the cord, but the drill plus the work light pushed the total past the cord's rating. Size for the combined load, or run a second cord from a different outlet.

Step two: match the gauge to amps and length

Wire thickness is measured in AWG, American Wire Gauge, and the scale runs backwards: a lower number means a thicker, heavier-duty wire. A 16-gauge cord is thin and light, fine for lamps and phone chargers. A 12-gauge cord is thick and stiff, built for serious tools. Memorise this one rule and you will never buy the wrong cord again.

Length matters as much as thickness because every extra foot adds resistance. A cord that safely carries 13 amps over 25 feet may only be safe for 10 amps at 100 feet. As a practical guide for common household use: 16 AWG suits light loads up to about 10 amps on short runs; 14 AWG covers medium-duty tools up to 15 amps on runs up to 50 feet; 12 AWG handles heavy tools up to 15 to 20 amps and stays safe at 100 feet; 10 AWG is the choice for long runs with heavy loads, like a compressor or welder at the far end of a driveway.

When in doubt, go one gauge thicker. There is no penalty for oversizing a cord except a little extra weight and cost, while undersizing costs you tool life at best and safety at worst. If you own exactly one extension cord, make it a 12-gauge, 50-foot, grounded cord: it covers nearly every realistic household job, from power tools to holiday lights, without ever being the weak link.

Reading the jacket: what the letters mean

Quality cords have their specifications printed or embossed along the jacket, and that string of letters is worth decoding. Take a common marking like 14 AWG 3C SJTW. The 14 AWG is the gauge. The 3C means three conductors: hot, neutral, and ground, which tells you the cord has a three-prong grounded plug. SJTW breaks down further: S for service cord, J for junior (300-volt rather than 600-volt insulation, standard for household cords), T for thermoplastic insulation, and W for weather-resistant, meaning it is rated for outdoor use.

That last letter is the one people ignore most dangerously. Indoor cords are not built for sun, rain, or temperature swings; their jackets crack and degrade outdoors. If a cord will see the outside of the house for any job, buy one explicitly rated for outdoor use, and look for a UL or ETL listing mark, which means an independent lab actually tested it.

Two-prong cords, with no ground pin, are fine for double-insulated tools and lamps, which are designed to be safe without a ground. But anything with a metal housing and a three-prong plug must be fed by a three-conductor grounded cord, and the ground pin must never be cut off or defeated with an adapter. That pin is the path that trips the breaker if the tool's wiring ever faults to its case.

Safety rules that actually matter

First, extension cords are for temporary use. Running one as a permanent substitute for proper wiring — across the garage to a freezer, year after year — is both a code violation and a slow-motion hazard. If a location needs power permanently, have an electrician install a real outlet there; it is cheaper than replacing the freezer and far cheaper than a fire.

Second, never daisy-chain cords end to end to gain length. Each connection adds resistance and a point where a loose fit can arc and overheat, and the total length quickly pushes you into voltage-drop territory. Buy or borrow a single cord of the right length instead. Related to this: never run a cord under a rug, through a doorway that pinches it, or coiled tightly while under heavy load. A coiled cord cannot shed heat, and under a rug nobody will notice the insulation cooking until it is too late.

Third, inspect before every use and retire damaged cords immediately. Look for cracked or cut jackets, exposed copper, loose or wobbly prongs, and plugs that feel warm during use. A warm plug is telling you the connection is degrading; stop using the cord. Outdoor work adds one more rule: plug the cord into a GFCI-protected outlet, which cuts power in milliseconds if current leaks through water or damaged insulation to ground. Most modern exterior outlets have GFCI protection built in, but older ones may not, in which case a portable GFCI adapter is a cheap fix.

Common mistakes and when to call an electrician

The classic mistake is powering a space heater or window air conditioner through a light-duty cord, or worse, through a cord plus a power strip. Heating and cooling appliances draw near the full capacity of a 15-amp circuit all by themselves; they belong plugged directly into the wall, never into an undersized cord. If the wall outlet is too far away, the answer is an electrician, not a longer cord.

Another common error is matching the cord to the outlet instead of the tool. A 15-amp outlet does not make every cord safe on it: a thin 18-gauge cord will happily plug into that outlet and then overheat feeding a 12-amp saw. The breaker protects the house wiring, not the cord, so the cord has to be sized for the load on its own merits.

Finally, treat recurring problems as wiring problems, not cord problems. If a breaker trips every time you run a tool on a properly sized cord, the circuit is overloaded or the tool is failing, and a bigger cord will not fix either. If you find yourself permanently dependent on extension cords in a room, or you smell hot plastic near an outlet, stop and call a licensed electrician. Cords are a bridge to get power where you need it today; permanent wiring is the destination.

⚠️ Safety first: Electrical work can be dangerous. Always turn off power at the breaker, verify with a tester, and follow local codes. When in doubt, call a licensed electrician.
