Leaded vs Lead-Free Solder: Which Should You Use?

Nov 7, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
For hobby electronics, leaded 63/37 solder is the easier choice: it melts at 183°C (361°F), flows smoothly, and makes shiny, reliable joints at iron temperatures of 315-350°C (600-662°F). Lead-free SAC305 solder melts hotter at 217°C (423°F), needs an iron set to 350-400°C (662-752°F), and is harder to work with — but it is legally required for any product sold in the EU, California, and most commercial markets under RoHS rules. If you are learning, repairing your own gear, or building one-off projects for yourself, start with leaded solder; if you plan to sell what you build, learn lead-free from day one. This guide compares the two alloys head to head, with the real numbers, the safety rules, and the exact temperatures for each.

What the two solders actually are

Leaded solder for electronics is almost always a tin-lead alloy, and the standard is 63/37: 63% tin and 37% lead. That exact ratio is special because it is eutectic, meaning it melts and solidifies at a single temperature, 183°C (361°F), with no pasty in-between stage. The older 60/40 blend is nearly identical in practice, melting across a narrow 183-190°C (361-374°F) range, but 63/37 is the one experienced builders reach for because it freezes instantly, which is exactly what you want when a joint must not move while it cools. A 1-pound spool of 0.031-inch (0.8 mm) 63/37 wire costs roughly $25-50 depending on brand, and a single 1-ounce tube like the Kester 44 runs about $10 and lasts a beginner months.

Lead-free solder replaces the lead with silver and copper. The industry standard is SAC305: 96.5% tin, 3.0% silver, and 0.5% copper. It melts at 217°C (423°F), which is 34°C hotter than leaded solder, and it has a small pasty range of about 217-220°C. That silver content is not decorative — it improves wetting and joint strength — but it also makes SAC305 noticeably more expensive: expect roughly 2-3 times the price of the same weight of leaded wire, with a 500-gram spool of name-brand SAC305 running $100-135. Both types of wire carry a rosin flux core, usually 2-3% by weight, which cleans the metal surfaces as you solder; the flux is what makes the solder flow, not the alloy itself.

Melting points and iron temperatures compared

The 34°C gap in melting points drives almost every practical difference between the two solders. Your iron must run hotter for lead-free work, and the margin for error shrinks: too cool and the solder will not wet the joint, too hot and you scorch the flux, lift PCB pads, and cook components. Two practical notes follow from those numbers. First, if your iron is a fixed-temperature 30-watt pencil with no adjustment, it will handle leaded solder comfortably but will struggle with lead-free joints on anything larger than a small pad — a temperature-controlled station that holds 350-400°C is close to mandatory for SAC305. Second, lead-free solder punishes hesitation: because it freezes through a pasty range instead of instantly, a joint that moves even slightly while cooling can turn into a grainy, unreliable cold joint. Hold the work perfectly still for 2-3 seconds after removing the iron, which is about twice as long as leaded solder needs.

How they solder differently in practice

The first thing a beginner notices is the look of the joint. A good leaded joint is bright, mirror-shiny, and smoothly concave where the solder meets the pin and pad. A good lead-free joint is noticeably duller and slightly grainy even when it is perfect. This trips up almost everyone switching alloys: they keep reheating a sound SAC305 joint trying to make it shine, and each extra second of heat damages the board a little more. Learn to judge lead-free joints by shape — smooth fillet, full wetting up the pin, no cracks — not by shine.

Wetting is the second difference. Leaded solder spreads eagerly across clean copper and tinned pads; lead-free solder is more reluctant and needs clean surfaces, fresh flux, and the right temperature to wet properly. On large ground planes and thick wires the difference is stark: leaded solder will flow into a heavy joint in 2-3 seconds, while SAC305 may need 4-6 seconds and a 60-watt or stronger iron to deliver enough heat. If you do a lot of work on ground planes, thick speaker wire, or large connectors, leaded solder saves real frustration.

The third difference is tip wear. Lead-free alloys dissolve iron plating faster, so soldering iron tips wear out sooner — roughly 30-50% faster by most workshop estimates — and they oxidize more readily at the higher temperatures involved. Keep the tip tinned at all times, clean it on brass wool rather than a wet sponge, and expect to replace tips more often. None of this makes lead-free solder bad; it is the standard for all commercial electronics for a reason, and its joints are mechanically strong and reliable once you learn its habits. It simply has a steeper learning curve.

Health, safety, and the law

Lead is toxic, and that fact deserves a straight answer rather than panic. Soldering with leaded wire does not produce lead vapor at normal iron temperatures — lead boils at 1,749°C (3,180°F), far above any soldering temperature — so the smoke rising from your iron is vaporized rosin flux, not lead. The real exposure route is handling: lead transfers to your hands from the wire, then to your mouth if you eat, drink, or touch your face without washing. The rule is simple and non-negotiable: wash your hands with soap after every soldering session, never eat at the bench, and keep leaded solder away from children. With those habits, hobby use of leaded solder is low-risk, which is why it remains legal for personal use almost everywhere.

The fumes are the same for both alloys and deserve more attention than they usually get. Rosin flux smoke irritates eyes, nose, and lungs, and long-term daily exposure is linked to occupational asthma in professional assemblers. Work in a ventilated room, put a small fan behind your bench to blow smoke away from your face, and consider a benchtop fume extractor — a $30-50 filtered fan unit — if you solder for more than an hour at a time. This matters equally for lead-free work, because the flux, not the lead, is what you are breathing.

The legal picture is where lead-free solder becomes mandatory. The EU RoHS directive has restricted lead in electrical and electronic equipment sold in Europe since 2006, and California's RoHS law mirrors it; similar rules apply in China, Japan, and a growing list of other markets. If you sell a kit, a finished device, or even a repaired product commercially, it must be lead-free in those markets. Personal projects, repairs of your own equipment, and hobby builds are exempt. So the rule of thumb is clean: build for yourself with whichever alloy you prefer, but build for sale with SAC305 and label your work accordingly.

Which should you buy? A decision guide

Start with your goal. If you are learning to solder, fixing your own headphones, or building Arduino and Raspberry Pi projects for your own bench, buy leaded 63/37 solder in 0.031-inch (0.8 mm) diameter. It is cheaper, more forgiving, and your first hundred joints will simply look and feel better, which matters when you are building confidence. A 1-ounce tube of Kester 44 is enough for months of beginner projects, and the skills transfer directly: every technique you learn — tinning, heat the work not the solder, hold still while it cools — works the same with lead-free later.

If you plan to sell kits or finished products, or you work for a company that ships hardware, choose lead-free SAC305 from the start and accept the learning curve. Set your iron to 350-375°C for ordinary PCB work, keep a roll of 0.5-0.8 mm wire on hand, and practice on scrap boards until dull-but-sound joints stop bothering you. Budget for faster tip wear and a slightly hotter, more capable station — the 60-75 watt temperature-controlled irons that beginners already buy for leaded work are exactly right.

Can you keep both on the bench? Yes, and many experienced builders do: leaded for personal repairs and prototyping, lead-free for anything that might be sold. Just keep the spools clearly labeled and never mix the alloys in the same joint — mixing creates an unpredictable alloy with a wide, slushy melting range that makes weak, unreliable joints. If you are switching a board from one alloy to the other, remove the old solder completely with braid or a solder sucker first. One last practical tip: buy name-brand wire from Kester, Weller, or MG Chemicals rather than the cheapest unbranded spool. Bad flux and impure alloys cause more beginner frustration than any technique mistake, and a $10 tube of good solder is the cheapest upgrade in the whole hobby.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is leaded solder illegal?

No, leaded solder is legal to buy and use for personal projects and repairs in the US and most countries. It is restricted only in products sold commercially: the EU RoHS directive and California RoHS law ban lead in electrical equipment placed on the market. Hobby use, personal repairs, and one-off builds for yourself are exempt.

Can you mix leaded and lead-free solder?

You should not mix them in the same joint. Mixing creates an unpredictable alloy with a wide melting range, which produces weak, grainy joints that are prone to cracking. If you are switching a board from one alloy to the other, remove all the old solder first with desoldering braid or a solder sucker, then solder with the new alloy.

Why do my lead-free solder joints look dull?

Dull, slightly grainy joints are normal for lead-free SAC305 solder, even when the joint is perfect. Leaded solder freezes shiny, but lead-free solder has a different crystal structure that always looks matte. Judge lead-free joints by their shape — a smooth fillet that wets fully up the pin with no cracks — not by shine, and do not keep reheating a sound joint trying to make it glossy.

What temperature should I set my iron for lead-free solder?

Set a temperature-controlled iron to 350-375°C (662-707°F) for ordinary PCB work with SAC305, and up to 400°C (752°F) for large ground planes or thick wires. Lead-free solder melts at 217°C, so it needs roughly 35-50°C more iron temperature than leaded 63/37, which works at 315-350°C. A fixed-temperature pencil iron usually cannot hold enough heat for lead-free work on anything but small pads.

Is lead-free solder safer than leaded solder?

Lead-free solder removes the lead-exposure risk from handling the wire, but it is not automatically safer overall: both alloys use rosin flux, and the flux smoke is the main thing you breathe while soldering. Ventilation and a fume extractor matter equally for both. With leaded solder, the key safety habit is washing your hands after every session and never eating at the bench, since lead transfers by touch rather than by vapor.
