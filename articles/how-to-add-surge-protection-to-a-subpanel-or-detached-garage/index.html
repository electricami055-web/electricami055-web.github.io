How to Add Surge Protection to a Subpanel or Detached Garage

Nov 2, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
Yes, a subpanel more than about 30 feet from the main panel, or in a detached garage or workshop, should have its own surge protector: a Type 2 device rated 80 to 108 kA, wired to a dedicated two-pole breaker in the subpanel, typically costing $150 to $400 installed by a licensed electrician. The whole-house protector at the main panel guards the service entrance, but it cannot fully cover a long feeder run that acts as an antenna for lightning-induced surges, or equipment fed from a panel in a separate building. A second protector at the subpanel closes that gap, and for a detached building it is the layer that protects the EV charger, the garage-door opener, and the workshop tools that the main-panel unit never sees.

Why the main-panel protector does not cover a distant subpanel

A surge protector can only clamp what reaches it. When a surge enters through the utility service, the main-panel device diverts it before it travels through the house wiring. But a subpanel fed by a long cable run has two exposures the main-panel unit cannot fix. First, a lightning strike near the property can induce a surge directly onto the feeder cable between the buildings, so the spike arrives at the subpanel without ever passing the main panel. Second, surges generated inside the subpanel's own circuits, such as a large compressor, welder, or EV charger switching on or off, start downstream of the main-panel protector and travel straight to the equipment on that panel.

Distance makes both problems worse. As a rule of thumb used by surge-protection guides, a subpanel more than 10 to 15 metres (roughly 30 to 50 feet) from the main panel, or in a separate building, deserves its own device. The long cable run adds impedance, which means the voltage that finally reaches your equipment is higher than the voltage the main-panel unit clamped to. The same logic applies inside the house: a basement subpanel feeding a home office, or a second-floor panel feeding a theatre room, protects expensive gear that sits far from the main panel's clamp point.

None of this means the main-panel protector was a waste of money. Layered protection is how commercial buildings do it, and it is the right model for a home: the service-entrance device handles the big external events, the subpanel device handles what arrives on the feeder or starts on its own circuits, and point-of-use strips catch whatever is left at the most sensitive gear. Skipping the middle layer leaves the most exposed panel in the house, the one in the detached garage, as the least protected.

Which device belongs at a subpanel

For almost every residential subpanel, the right device is a Type 2 SPD wired into the subpanel itself, the same class of device used at the main panel. Type 2 units are designed to be installed on the load side of the service, they connect to a dedicated two-pole breaker, and they are available from every major panel brand. Type 1 devices, which mount at the service entrance ahead of the main breaker, belong at the main service, not at a subpanel, although some Type 1 units are dual-rated and can be wired at a subpanel too. Check the listing before buying: the device must be UL 1449 listed for the location you are putting it in.

External-mount units are the most forgiving choice for a subpanel. They mount beside the panel through a knockout, they fit any brand of load centre, and their NEMA 4X enclosure handles a dusty garage or an exterior wall without a second enclosure. Breaker-style units are neater when the panel has two spare slots, but they only fit their own brand's bus system, so a Siemens breaker unit will not plug into a Square D panel. Whatever style you choose, confirm the voltage: a standard US home subpanel is 120/240 V single-phase, and the SPD must match it. A three-phase unit, or a 120 V-only plug-in device, is not a substitute.

One more distinction matters for a detached building: the subpanel SPD is not a lightning-protection system. No residential SPD stops a direct lightning strike, at any rating. What it does is handle nearby strikes, utility switching, and restoration surges, which are the events that actually destroy most garage and workshop electronics. If your property takes frequent direct strikes, talk to your electrician about a dedicated lightning-protection system in addition to, not instead of, the SPD.

Sizing it right: kA, VPR, breaker space, and the neutral-ground rule

Size a subpanel SPD the same way you size a main-panel one: by surge current capacity, not by the panel's amperage. A 100-amp subpanel does not need a smaller SPD than a 200-amp main panel, because the kA rating describes the surge the device can divert, not the load it serves. For a typical garage or workshop subpanel, 80 kA is the mainstream pick, and 108 kA is the sensible step up if the building is in a high-lightning region, feeds an EV charger, or holds expensive equipment. Anything below about 50 kA is undersized for panel duty. Then check the voltage protection rating (VPR): 600 volts line-to-neutral or lower is the quality benchmark, and the nominal discharge current (In) should be at least 10 kA, with 20 kA better for panels that see frequent utility-switching surges.

Breaker space decides the mounting style. A breaker-wired Type 2 needs a dedicated two-pole breaker, and Eaton's own instructions call for a 50-amp two-pole breaker to achieve the CHSPT2ULTRA's full published 108 kA rating, although a 15-amp two-pole breaker is the minimum it accepts. If the subpanel is full, an external-mount unit that wires to an existing breaker position or a small external enclosure avoids a panel upgrade, and your electrician can advise which route is cheaper. Lead length matters as much as the device: the SPD should sit as close to the subpanel as possible, with short, straight leads, because every extra inch of wire adds impedance that lets more surge voltage through.

The safety-critical wiring rule for subpanels is neutral-ground separation. In a main panel, the neutral and ground bars are bonded together; in a subpanel, they must stay separate, with no bonding jumper, and the ground bar connected back to the main panel plus, for a detached building, to its own grounding electrodes. An SPD has four connections, two hot legs, neutral, and ground, and the ground wire goes to the subpanel's ground bar while the neutral wire goes to its neutral bar. Getting this wrong defeats the protection and creates a shock hazard, which is one more reason this is electrician work, not a first DIY panel project. If your subpanel currently has neutral and ground on the same bar, flag it to your electrician before adding anything.

What the install actually involves, step by step

A straightforward subpanel SPD install takes about one to two hours, and it follows the same sequence as a main-panel install. The electrician switches off the subpanel's feeder breaker at the main panel and verifies the subpanel is dead with a tester, then removes the panel cover. The critical safety warning: switching off the main breaker does not de-energize everything inside a panel. The service conductors feeding the panel stay live, and inside a subpanel the feeder lugs stay live unless the feeder breaker at the main panel is off. This is why panel work injures experienced people who get casual about it. If you are unsure about any part of this, hire a licensed electrician and stay out of the panel.

With the panel safe, the electrician mounts the device, either beside the panel through a knockout for an external unit, or into two spare breaker slots for a breaker-style unit, then runs its leads to the dedicated two-pole breaker and to the neutral and ground bars, keeping the leads as short and straight as possible. The breaker is switched on, the device's indicator LEDs are checked, and the cover goes back on. A green LED means the unit is protecting; a dark LED after a storm means it has sacrificed itself and must be replaced, even though the panel's power still works. For a detached garage, the electrician also confirms the building's grounding electrodes, typically two 8-foot ground rods, are present and connected, since a separate building requires its own grounding electrode system under the National Electrical Code.

After the install, the homeowner's job is simple: check the LEDs after every major storm, and budget for a replacement every 5 to 10 years as the metal-oxide varistors inside wear down from repeated smaller surges. Write the install date on the panel door with a marker so the next person knows how old the unit is. And keep the layering in mind: the subpanel SPD protects the panel's circuits, but a $25 to $60 point-of-use unit at the workbench computer, the network switch, or the home-theatre rack is still worth it for the gear you would most hate to replace.

Cost, and the mistakes that waste the money

A subpanel SPD project usually lands in the same range as a main-panel install: $150 to $400 all-in for a straightforward job, with the device itself at $70 to $130 and the electrician's labour at roughly $150 to $300 for one to two hours. Detached garages can run higher if the panel needs an outdoor-rated enclosure, if the feeder run needs trenching for a new grounding electrode, or if the panel is full and needs a small sub-feed or breaker rearrangement. Get the quote itemized, device plus labour, and compare it against the replacement cost of what the panel feeds: one fried EV charger board or garage-door opener control board runs $300 to $1,200, which is why the maths usually favours installing the device.

The most common mistake is buying the device and mounting it far from the panel on long, coiled leads, which quietly destroys its performance. The second is installing it in a subpanel where neutral and ground are incorrectly bonded, which must be fixed first. The third is assuming one device covers the whole property: the main-panel SPD, the subpanel SPD, and point-of-use units are layers, and each layer you skip is a path a surge can take. The fourth is forgetting the device exists: an SPD with a dark LED is a plastic box, not protection, and the only way to know is to look at it after storms.

If you only remember one thing, make it this: a subpanel is a panel, and panels deserve the same protection as the main one, sized by the same rules and installed by the same licensed electrician. A distant garage or workshop panel is more exposed than the main panel, not less, because of the long feeder feeding it. Closing that gap with an 80 to 108 kA Type 2 device, wired tight to the subpanel with its neutral and ground on the correct bars, is one of the cheapest insurance policies in a home electrical system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a subpanel need its own surge protector if the main panel already has one?

Yes, if the subpanel is more than about 30 to 50 feet from the main panel or in a separate building. Surges can be induced directly onto the long feeder cable, bypassing the main-panel device entirely, and surges generated on the subpanel's own circuits start downstream of it. A dedicated Type 2 SPD at the subpanel closes both gaps. A subpanel a few feet from the main panel, feeding only basic loads, can reasonably rely on the main-panel unit alone.

Can I install a surge protector in a subpanel myself?

Only if you are confident working inside a live panel, and most homeowners should not. Switching off the subpanel's breaker does not de-energize the feeder lugs, and the service conductors stay live even with the main breaker off. A licensed electrician typically completes a subpanel SPD install in one to two hours for $150 to $300 in labour, and the work also lets them verify the neutral-ground separation and grounding electrodes, which are easy to get wrong.

What size surge protector do I need for a 100-amp subpanel?

The kA rating is independent of the panel's amperage, so a 100-amp subpanel takes the same 80 to 108 kA Type 2 device as a 200-amp main panel. Choose 80 kA for a typical garage or workshop, and 108 kA for high-lightning regions or panels feeding an EV charger or expensive equipment. Check that the voltage protection rating is 600 volts line-to-neutral or lower and that the unit is UL 1449 listed.

Does a detached garage need its own surge protector and ground rods?

Yes to both. A detached building needs its own grounding electrode system, typically two 8-foot ground rods, under the National Electrical Code, and its subpanel needs its own SPD because the long feeder run from the house acts as an antenna for lightning-induced surges. The SPD's ground wire connects to the subpanel's ground bar, which ties to those electrodes, while its neutral wire goes to the separate neutral bar. Have your electrician verify both before adding the device.

Where does a subpanel surge protector connect, the ground bar or the neutral bar?

Both, on separate wires. A panel SPD has four connections: two hot legs to a dedicated two-pole breaker, a neutral wire to the subpanel's neutral bar, and a ground wire to its ground bar. In a subpanel the neutral and ground bars must stay separate, with no bonding jumper, unlike a main panel where they are bonded. If your subpanel has neutral and ground mixed on one bar, that wiring fault must be corrected before the SPD goes in.
