Why Do My Lights Flicker? Causes, Fixes, and When to Call an Electrician

Oct 14, 2026 · 8 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
A flickering light is one of the most common electrical complaints homeowners have — and one of the most confusing. Sometimes it is nothing more than a loose bulb or an LED on the wrong dimmer. Other times it is loose wiring or a supply problem that should never be ignored. This guide walks you through every common cause, from the five-minute fixes to the red flags that mean it is time to call a licensed electrician.

Why Lights Flicker: The Short Explanation

A flickering light is almost always a sign that the flow of electricity to the bulb is being interrupted or is fluctuating. Somewhere between your breaker panel and the filament, the connection is loose, the voltage is dipping, or the bulb's electronics are struggling to cope with what they are being fed.

The important thing to understand is that flicker is a symptom, not a diagnosis. A single lamp that blinks is usually a five-minute bulb or switch problem. Every light in the house flickering at once is a completely different matter — it points to something upstream, like your panel, the wiring, or even the utility supply.

Learning to tell the difference matters, because one kind of flicker is a nuisance and the other can be a fire hazard. This guide walks you through the causes from most common to most serious, what you can safely check yourself, and the red flags that mean you should stop and call a licensed electrician.

Start With the Easy Stuff: Bulb, Socket, and Switch

Before you suspect anything dramatic, rule out the simple causes — they account for the majority of flickering-light complaints. Turn the light off and let the bulb cool completely, then twist it firmly into its socket. A bulb that is even slightly loose makes intermittent contact, and that is all it takes for a flicker.

If tightening does not fix it, swap in a new bulb of the correct type and wattage. Bulbs fail gradually: an LED's driver electronics or a filament can start to break down weeks before the bulb dies completely, producing a flicker as a warning sign. Swapping the bulb is the fastest diagnostic you have — if the new one is steady, you are done.

Still flickering? Look at the socket itself. In screw-base fixtures, a small metal tab at the bottom of the socket carries current to the bulb, and over years of heat cycling it can lose its spring tension or corrode. With the power off at the switch (and ideally at the breaker), you can gently bend that tab upward a millimetre or two to restore firm contact. If the socket is cracked, scorched, or badly corroded, replace the fixture or have an electrician do it.

Finally, pay attention to the switch. A worn-out light switch with tired internal contacts can produce exactly the same flicker, especially if it feels loose, warm, or makes a crackling sound when you flip it. Replacing a switch is a routine DIY job for many homeowners, but only if you are comfortable turning off the breaker and verifying the circuit is dead with a non-contact voltage tester first. When in doubt, call a professional.

LED Bulbs and Dimmer Switches: The Most Common Modern Cause

If your flickering light is an LED on a dimmer, you have probably already found the culprit. Most older dimmer switches were designed for incandescent bulbs, which are simple resistive loads. LEDs are electronic loads that behave very differently, and an old leading-edge (phase-cut) dimmer feeding an LED will almost always cause flicker, buzzing, strobing, or lights that turn off below a certain dim level.

The fix is compatibility. First, check that the bulb is actually dimmable — a surprising number of flicker complaints come down to non-dimmable LEDs installed on a dimmer circuit. Second, match the bulb to a dimmer that is rated for LED loads, ideally a trailing-edge dimmer listed as LED-compatible. Many dimmer manufacturers publish compatibility charts for specific LED models, and checking that list before you buy saves a lot of frustration.

A related quirk: some LEDs glow faintly or flicker even when switched off. This is usually caused by a small leakage current through an illuminated switch or a smart switch that has no neutral wire — the tiny current is not enough to light the bulb properly, so it pulses or ghosts. The solutions range from swapping to an LED with better internal filtering to installing a snubber (a small resistor-capacitor module wired across the load) or switching to a dimmer or smart switch that includes a neutral connection.

Low-quality LEDs deserve a mention too. Cheap bulbs with poor driver electronics produce invisible high-frequency flicker that can cause headaches or eye strain in sensitive people. If a flickering LED is also giving you headaches, replace it with a bulb from a reputable brand rated for low flicker — the difference is measurable and often worth the extra few dollars.

Voltage Dips and Overloaded Circuits: When Appliances Kick On

Do your lights dip or flicker for a second whenever the refrigerator compressor starts, the air conditioner kicks in, or someone runs the microwave? That is a voltage dip. Large motors draw a surge of current when they start, and if the lighting circuit shares that draw, the voltage momentarily sags — enough for you to see it in the lights.

An occasional brief dip when a big appliance starts is normal and mostly harmless. But if it happens constantly, the lights dim noticeably, or the dip has got worse over time, it can indicate that a circuit is overloaded or that the wiring is undersized for the load. Heavy continuous loading heats conductors, and heat is the enemy of safe wiring.

The practical remedies are straightforward. Move power-hungry portable appliances (space heaters, window air conditioners, microwaves) onto circuits that do not also feed your lighting. For permanently wired equipment, a licensed electrician can install dedicated circuits so motor loads and lighting are separated. If the flicker appeared after you added a new appliance, that is a strong hint about where the load problem sits.

If lights across the whole house flicker or dim when a large appliance runs — not just one circuit — the problem may be at your service entrance, the main panel, or even the utility supply. Loose service conductors or a failing utility transformer can produce exactly this pattern. This is not a DIY situation: call an electrician, and if they confirm the issue is upstream of your meter, contact your power company.

Loose Connections: The Dangerous Kind You Cannot See

Some flicker is caused by loose wiring connections hidden inside junction boxes, behind outlets, or at the breaker panel. Unlike a loose bulb, you cannot see these, and they are the ones that start fires. A loose terminal screw or a splice that was never made up properly creates a high-resistance point; current passing through it generates heat, the heat accelerates oxidation and loosening, and in bad cases the connection arcs — tiny sustained sparks that can reach thousands of degrees.

There are warning signs worth taking seriously. Flickering that affects a whole circuit, or lights in different rooms that blink together, suggests a loose connection at a shared point — a junction box, a wire nut, or a breaker terminal. Buzzing or crackling sounds from outlets, switches, or the panel, warmth or discolouration around outlets and switch plates, and a burning plastic smell are all signs that a connection is overheating right now.

Homes with older wiring deserve extra attention. Knob-and-tube and early aluminium wiring, degraded insulation, and boxes where copper and aluminium conductors meet without proper rated connectors all raise the risk of connection problems. If your flickering started after a renovation — when wires were moved, cut, or re-terminated — have the affected circuits inspected.

Here is the firm safety line: tightening a terminal inside a breaker panel or reworking splices in a junction box is not a beginner DIY task. It means working inches from energised conductors, and a mistake can injure you or damage the panel. Turn off the breaker feeding the affected circuit so nothing gets worse, and then call a licensed electrician to open things up and inspect. Arc-fault circuit interrupter (AFCI) breakers exist precisely to catch arcing faults like these, so ask your electrician whether your panel would benefit from them.

When to Stop Troubleshooting and Call an Electrician

You can safely handle the basics — tightening and replacing bulbs, swapping to dimmable LEDs, and replacing a worn switch if you are confident working with the breaker off. Everything beyond that belongs to a professional. Here are the red flags that mean you should stop and call one now.

Call a licensed electrician promptly if: multiple lights or whole circuits flicker together; the flicker started suddenly with no change to bulbs or switches; breakers trip when the lights flicker; you hear buzzing, crackling, or sizzling from outlets, switches, or the panel; outlets or switch plates feel warm or look scorched; you smell burning plastic; or the flicker affects your neighbours too, which points to a utility-side fault.

In an emergency — visible sparking, smoke, or a burning smell — do not touch anything electrical. Turn off the main breaker only if you can reach it safely, leave the area, and call your fire department if there is any sign of fire, then your electrician.

One last note for the cost-conscious: an inspection for flickering lights is usually a short service call, and catching a loose connection early is enormously cheaper than repairing fire damage. Flicker that is getting worse is a system telling you something is degrading. Listen to it sooner rather than later.

⚠️ Safety first: Electrical work can be dangerous. Always turn off power at the breaker, verify with a tester, and follow local codes. When in doubt, call a licensed electrician.
