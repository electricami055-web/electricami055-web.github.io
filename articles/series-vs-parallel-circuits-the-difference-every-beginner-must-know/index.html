Wiring Basics

Series vs Parallel Circuits: The Difference Every Beginner Must Know

Oct 5, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
Every circuit you will ever build connects its components in one of two ways: series or parallel. Mix the two up and your LEDs glow dim, your resistors run hot, or your calculations come out wrong. This guide makes the difference intuitive, gives you the only maths you need, and walks you through building both types on a breadboard.

The single idea that separates them: how many paths does the current have?

Forget the textbook definitions for a moment. There is exactly one question that tells series and parallel apart: how many paths can the electric current take?

In a series circuit, components are connected end to end in a single loop, like carriages on a train. Current has one path. It leaves the battery, flows through the first component, then the second, then the third, and returns to the battery. Every electron passes through every component.

In a parallel circuit, components sit on separate branches between the same two points, like lanes on a motorway. Current splits at the junction, takes whichever branches are available, and rejoins on the other side. Each branch carries its own share of the current.

That one difference — one path versus many paths — determines everything else: how voltage behaves, how current behaves, what happens when something fails, and how you calculate total resistance. Once you can look at a wiring diagram and trace the paths, the rest of the rules fall into place.

Series circuits: one loop, same current, split voltage

In a series circuit, the current is identical through every component. There is nowhere else for it to go, so whatever current leaves the battery must pass through component one, then component two, in turn. Measure the current at any point in the loop and you get the same reading.

Voltage, on the other hand, gets divided. The battery's voltage is shared among the components in proportion to their resistance. Put two identical bulbs in series across a 9-volt battery and each bulb gets roughly 4.5 volts. Add a third identical bulb and each gets about 3 volts — which is why each one glows dimmer than before.

Resistance adds up simply: total resistance equals the sum of the individual resistances (R_total = R1 + R2 + R3). More components in series means more total resistance, which means less current flows from the battery overall. This is the behaviour to remember when you stack batteries or string components in a chain.

The defining weakness of series wiring is also its most famous feature: one break kills everything. Open the loop anywhere — a failed bulb, a loose wire, a blown fuse — and current stops everywhere. Old-style Christmas lights were wired this way, which is why one dead bulb darkened the whole string and everyone spent December hunting for it.

Parallel circuits: branches, same voltage, split current

Parallel circuits flip the rules. Every branch connects across the same two points, so every component sees the full supply voltage. Two bulbs in parallel across a 9-volt battery each get the full 9 volts, and adding a third bulb does not dim the first two. Each branch behaves as if the others were not there.

Current, meanwhile, divides. The total current leaving the battery equals the sum of the currents in each branch. If one branch draws 20 milliamps and another draws 30, the battery supplies 50. Adding more branches draws more total current from the supply — something to keep in mind when sizing batteries and power supplies.

Total resistance in parallel follows the reciprocal rule: 1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3. The result is always less than the smallest individual resistance. Two 100-ohm resistors in parallel give 50 ohms; three give about 33. This surprises most beginners, but it makes physical sense: each new branch is another path for current, so the overall circuit conducts more easily.

The defining strength of parallel wiring is independence. If one branch opens, the others keep working. That is why your house is wired in parallel: switching off one lamp does not kill the television, and every outlet delivers the full mains voltage regardless of what is plugged in elsewhere.

The only maths you need: Ohm's law and two resistance rules

You need exactly three formulas, and you already know two of them. First, the resistance rules from the previous sections: in series, add the resistances (R_total = R1 + R2 + ...); in parallel, add the reciprocals (1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...). For the common case of two resistors in parallel, there is a shortcut: R_total = (R1 x R2) / (R1 + R2).

Second, Ohm's law: V = I x R. Voltage equals current times resistance. Rearranged, it gives you current (I = V / R) and resistance (R = V / I). With these you can predict what any simple circuit will do before you build it.

Try a worked example. Two 100-ohm resistors in series across a 9-volt battery: total resistance is 200 ohms, so current is 9 / 200 = 0.045 amps (45 milliamps), the same through both resistors, with 4.5 volts across each. Now the same two resistors in parallel: total resistance is 50 ohms, total current is 9 / 50 = 180 milliamps, and each branch carries 90 milliamps at the full 9 volts. Notice the parallel version draws four times the current from the battery — this is why parallel circuits drain batteries faster, and why you must check that your power supply can handle the total.

One more practical use: sizing the resistor for an LED. A typical red LED drops about 2 volts and wants about 20 milliamps. On a 9-volt supply, the resistor must drop the remaining 7 volts at 0.02 amps, so R = 7 / 0.02 = 350 ohms — the nearest standard value, 330 ohms, works perfectly. This single calculation covers most beginner LED projects.

Where each type shows up in real life

Once you know the two patterns, you start seeing them everywhere. Your home's electrical system is the biggest parallel circuit you own. Every outlet, light, and appliance connects across the same mains supply, each drawing what it needs independently. The breaker panel exists precisely because parallel branches can each draw heavy current: a breaker watches its branch and trips before the wiring overheats.

Fuses and switches, by contrast, must be in series with what they protect. A fuse sits in the single path feeding a circuit, so any fault current has to pass through it and blow it. A wall switch breaks the one path to the light. If a switch were wired in parallel, flipping it would short the supply instead of controlling the lamp — a classic and dangerous beginner error in mains wiring.

Batteries combine both ways. Cells in series add their voltages: two 1.5-volt AA cells in series give 3 volts, which is how most battery holders are wired. Cells in parallel keep the same voltage but add their capacities, lasting longer under the same load. Never mix old and new cells, and never parallel-connect batteries of different voltages — the stronger one will force current backward through the weaker one.

Old incandescent Christmas lights were series strings, which is why one dead bulb killed the whole strand. Modern LED strings are usually wired as several parallel groups, so a single failed LED only darkens its own section. Car headlights are parallel so one burnt-out bulb does not leave you driving blind. And inside electronics, pull-up resistors, LED arrays, and sensor networks all use deliberate series-parallel combinations to get exactly the voltage and current each part needs.

Build both on a breadboard in one afternoon

Reading about current paths is useful; feeling them is better. You need a solderless breadboard, a 9-volt battery with a snap connector, two LEDs, two 330-ohm resistors, and a handful of jumper wires. Total cost is a few pounds, and nothing here involves mains voltage — battery circuits at 9 volts are safe to experiment with freely.

Build the series version first. Connect the battery's positive terminal to a 330-ohm resistor, the resistor to the first LED's long leg (anode), the LED's short leg (cathode) to the second LED's anode, the second cathode to the second resistor, and the resistor back to battery negative. Both LEDs light, but dimmer than a single LED would be — they are sharing the 9 volts.

Now rebuild it in parallel. Run battery positive to a rail, battery negative to the other rail, then connect each LED-plus-resistor pair across the two rails on its own branch. Both LEDs glow at full brightness, and removing one LED leaves the other shining. That single experiment teaches more than a chapter of theory.

If you own a multimeter, measure the voltage across each LED in both builds and the current through each branch — you will see the series readings split the voltage and the parallel readings match it. Keep the battery disconnected while rearranging wires, double-check LED polarity before powering up, and if anything gets warm, disconnect immediately and look for a short. When you are done, you will read wiring diagrams with new eyes: trace the paths, and you will know exactly which rules apply.

Beginner mistakes to avoid

The most common error is wiring an LED with no resistor, often because a parallel diagram makes it look like the LED connects straight across the battery. It does — and without a resistor to limit current, the LED draws far more than its rated 20 milliamps and burns out in seconds. Every LED branch needs its own current-limiting resistor; one shared resistor for two parallel LEDs divides unevenly and is a classic way to kill them one at a time.

Second, beginners often think current gets "used up" as it flows around a series loop, expecting less current after each component. It does not: current is the same at every point in a series circuit. What drops is voltage — each component takes its share, and the shares add up to the supply voltage. Keep the two quantities straight and most confusion evaporates.

Third, measuring resistance with a multimeter while the circuit is powered. The meter's resistance mode applies its own small test current, so a live circuit gives nonsense readings and can damage the meter. Always disconnect power before measuring resistance, and measure components one at a time rather than in-circuit where parallel paths skew the reading.

Finally, know where the beginner sandbox ends. Everything in this guide uses low-voltage batteries, which are forgiving. Household mains wiring is not: it can injure or kill, and mistakes can start fires inside your walls. If a project touches anything connected to the mains — outlets, switches, breaker panels — turn the breaker off, verify with a non-contact tester, and call a licensed electrician whenever you are unsure. The series-parallel rules still apply up there, but the stakes do not forgive experimentation.

⚠️ Safety first: Electrical work can be dangerous. Always turn off power at the breaker, verify with a tester, and follow local codes. When in doubt, call a licensed electrician.
