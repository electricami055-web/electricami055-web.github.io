GFCI vs AFCI: What's the Difference and Where Is Each Required?

Sep 30, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
Two of the most important safety devices in your home look almost identical: both are breakers or outlets with little test buttons on them, and both can save your life. But a GFCI and an AFCI watch for completely different dangers. Mixing them up is one of the most common sources of confusion for homeowners, so this guide explains exactly what each one does, where the electrical code requires them, and how to tell which protection your own circuits have.

What a GFCI does: protection against electric shock

A ground fault circuit interrupter, or GFCI, exists for one purpose: to keep you from being electrocuted. It works by constantly comparing the current flowing out on the hot wire with the current returning on the neutral wire. In a healthy circuit those two numbers match exactly. If even a tiny amount of current, as little as 4 to 6 milliamps, leaks off to an unintended path, say through your body to a wet bathroom floor, the GFCI detects the imbalance and cuts power in about one-fortieth of a second.

That speed is the whole point. It takes far less current to stop a human heart than most people imagine, and a standard breaker will never notice a ground fault at all: 5 milliamps is thousands of times below what trips a 15-amp breaker. The GFCI is the only device standing between a fault and a shock in wet locations, which is why code requires it anywhere water and electricity might meet.

You have almost certainly used a GFCI without thinking about it. They are the outlets with the little Test and Reset buttons on their face, common in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, and outdoors. GFCIs can also live at the breaker panel, where a single GFCI breaker protects every outlet on its circuit. Either form does the same job; the difference is just where the protection is installed.

What an AFCI does: protection against electrical fires

An arc fault circuit interrupter, or AFCI, hunts a completely different danger: dangerous arcing in your wiring. An arc is electricity jumping across a gap, and it happens when wires are damaged, chewed by rodents, pierced by a nail, or loosened at a connection. The jumping current runs extremely hot, hot enough to ignite wood framing, insulation, and dust inside your walls, and it can smolder undetected for a long time before anything trips a normal breaker.

Standard breakers are nearly blind to arcs. A loose connection arcing inside a wall might draw only a few amps, far below the breaker's trip threshold, while generating thousands of degrees at the arc point. The AFCI solves this with electronics that listen to the circuit's waveform and recognize the distinctive signature of a dangerous arc, distinct from the harmless arcs made by light switches, motor brushes, and plugging things in. When it recognizes the bad pattern, it opens the circuit before the arc can start a fire.

AFCIs entered homes around 1999 and their required territory has expanded with every code cycle since. Unlike GFCIs, which usually sit at the outlet, AFCIs are almost always installed as breakers at the panel, because they need to guard the entire run of branch-circuit wiring, including the hidden wiring inside your walls where most arc faults begin.

GFCI vs AFCI: the key differences side by side

The one-sentence version: a GFCI protects people from shock, and an AFCI protects the house from fire. Everything else follows from that. A GFCI watches for current leaking to ground, trips at 4 to 6 milliamps, and reacts in milliseconds to save a life. An AFCI watches for the electrical signature of arcing in the wiring, and it exists to prevent the tens of thousands of home fires that damaged wiring causes.

They also live in different places for different reasons. GFCI protection is most often found at the outlet itself, because the danger it guards against, a person touching a faulty appliance near water, happens out in the room. AFCI protection is most often found at the breaker, because the danger it guards against, a nail through a cable or a loose wire nut, hides inside the walls, and only a device at the start of the circuit can see the whole run.

Here is the part that confuses people: they are not interchangeable, and one does not replace the other. A GFCI will not catch a dangerous arc inside your wall, and an AFCI will not save you from a shock in the bathtub. Many circuits need both kinds of protection at once, which is exactly why dual-function breakers exist: one device at the panel that provides GFCI and AFCI protection together. If your panel has breakers with test buttons and you are not sure which type they are, the tiny printed label on the breaker face, GFCI, AFCI, or CAFCI, or dual-function, tells you, and the breaker's color-coded test button often hints at it too.

Where each is required: a room-by-room guide

The National Electrical Code spells out where each type of protection is required, and the lists reflect what each device protects against. GFCI protection is required wherever water and electricity are likely to meet: bathrooms, kitchens (countertop receptacles), laundry areas, garages, unfinished basements, crawl spaces, outdoors, and within a certain distance of sinks, tubs, and pools. If the location is damp or wet, assume GFCI is required.

AFCI protection covers the living spaces where damaged wiring can smolder unnoticed: bedrooms, living rooms, dining rooms, family rooms, dens, hallways, closets, and similar habitable rooms. Kitchens and laundry areas often end up needing both types of protection on the same circuit, one against shock, one against fire, which is where dual-function breakers earn their keep.

Two important caveats. First, code requirements depend on which NEC edition your area adopted and when your home was built or last rewired; older homes are generally not required to retrofit everything to the newest code, though adding protection is always a smart upgrade. Second, local amendments can go further than the national code, so treat this guide as the baseline and check with your local building department or a licensed electrician before planning work.

Outlets, breakers, or dual-function: choosing the right form

For GFCI protection you have a real choice. A GFCI receptacle at the first outlet protects everything downstream wired through its load terminals, which is why one GFCI outlet can guard a whole string of ordinary bathroom outlets. A GFCI breaker at the panel protects the entire circuit from a single point and is easier to reset without hunting for the tripped outlet. Outlets cost less per point of protection; breakers are tidier when many locations on one circuit need coverage.

For AFCI protection the choice is mostly made for you: use an AFCI breaker at the panel. Outlet-type AFCIs exist but are rare, and the breaker form protects the home-run wiring back to the panel, which is the whole point. Where a circuit needs both, a dual-function AFCI/GFCI breaker is usually the cleanest answer, one device, one test button, both protections.

If you are replacing devices yourself, the non-negotiable safety ritual applies: turn off the breaker, verify dead with a non-contact tester, photograph and label every wire before disconnecting, and follow the device's line-versus-load markings exactly. GFCI outlets wired with line and load reversed look normal but protect nothing downstream. Breaker replacement means working inside the panel with the main still live, which is firmly electrician territory for most homeowners; the service lugs stay energized even with the main breaker off.

Testing, nuisance tripping, and when to call an electrician

Both devices have test buttons for a reason: press them monthly. A GFCI or AFCI that will not trip when tested, or will not reset afterward, has failed and must be replaced; a protection device that cannot trip is just an expensive regular outlet giving you false confidence. When either one trips in real use, treat it as information, not an annoyance, and find out why before resetting.

Nuisance tripping has different usual suspects for each type. A GFCI that trips randomly is often telling the truth: moisture in an outdoor box, a failing appliance with a small ground leak, or a downstream outlet wired to the wrong terminals. An AFCI that trips is often reacting to a real arc: a damaged cord, a loose connection, or sometimes an incompatible device like an older treadmill or a particular LED driver whose waveform mimics arcing. Unplug everything on the circuit and reconnect one device at a time to isolate the culprit.

Call a licensed electrician when a device trips repeatedly and you cannot find the cause, when you smell burning or see scorch marks, when the panel work involves anything beyond flipping breakers, or whenever you are unsure what you are looking at. Protection devices are your early warning system; an electrician is the response team. The service call costs far less than what either device is trying to prevent.

⚠️ Safety first: Electrical work can be dangerous. Always turn off power at the breaker, verify with a tester, and follow local codes. When in doubt, call a licensed electrician.
