What Size Whole-House Surge Protector Do You Need? kA Ratings Explained

Nov 1, 2026 · 9 min read · By ElectricTechs Team
For most US homes, an 80,000 to 140,000 amp (80–140 kA) per-phase Type 2 surge protector is the right size: 80 kA covers a typical suburban home, 100–108 kA suits homes with more electronics or moderate lightning exposure, and 140 kA is the pick for storm-prone regions and large homes with expensive gear. The kA rating is the maximum surge current the device can divert in a single event, not the amount of protection it gives every day, and anything below about 50 kA is undersized for whole-house duty in 2026. This guide explains what the rating really means, how to match it to your home, and the three other numbers on the spec sheet that matter just as much.

What the kA rating actually measures

The kA rating — short for kiloamps, or thousands of amps — is the surge current capacity per phase: the largest single surge current the SPD is rated to divert to ground without failing. A 140 kA device, like the Siemens FS140, is rated for 140,000 amps per phase; an 80 kA device, like the Square D HEPD80, for 80,000 amps. These are not currents your home normally sees. A typical household circuit carries 15 or 20 amps; a surge is a brief spike lasting microseconds, and the SPD's job is to clamp that spike and shunt the excess away before it reaches your electronics.

Two things the rating does not mean. First, it is not a score for everyday protection: a 140 kA unit and an 80 kA unit both clamp the same small daily surges from utility switching and appliances cycling, because both react in under a nanosecond and both clamp to roughly the same voltage. Second, it is not a lifetime total. An SPD absorbs many smaller surges over its 5- to 10-year life, and its metal-oxide varistors (MOVs) degrade a little with each one. The kA figure describes one big event, not the sum of everything it will ever handle. Think of it as the device's single-event headroom: bigger headroom means it survives bigger events and lasts longer under repeated medium ones.

Manufacturers sometimes quote the rating differently, so read the fine print. Some list surge current per phase, others per mode (line-to-neutral, line-to-ground), and a few add the modes together for a bigger headline number. The honest comparison is per-phase surge current capacity under UL 1449, which is the standard every legitimate residential SPD is listed to. If a listing only gives joules — the unit used for plug-in strips — it is not a whole-house device at all; joules measure energy absorbed by a strip, while kA measures current diverted by a panel device, and the two do not convert cleanly.

The sizing tiers: which kA rating fits which home

For a standard single-family home on a 100- or 200-amp panel, the practical tiers break down by exposure and equipment value rather than by square footage alone. A 40–50 kA unit is the entry tier: adequate for a small home or apartment with few sensitive electronics, but it leaves little headroom for a serious event, and most electricians now consider it the floor rather than a recommendation. An 80 kA unit is the mainstream pick — enough headroom for a typical suburban home, and the tier where prices are most competitive, often around $70 for the device alone.

The 100–108 kA tier is the sweet spot for many buyers: the price step up from 80 kA is usually only $20–$40, while the extra headroom covers homes with a home office, a home theatre, a heat pump, or an EV charger — loads whose control boards are exactly what surges destroy. The 140 kA tier is for homes where lightning is a regular event (Florida, the Gulf Coast, and the southern Plains see the highest strike density in the US), for large homes with 400-amp service or multiple subpanels, and for anyone whose replacement cost for one fried HVAC board ($400–$1,200) already exceeds the price difference. Siemens itself sizes its FirstSurge line by geography for this reason, offering 60 kA, 100 kA, and 140 kA models matched to regional thunderstorm frequency.

One rule of thumb electricians use: size for the worst surge you expect in the device's lifetime, not the average one. You are not buying capacity you will use daily; you are buying the margin that lets the device survive the one event that matters and keep protecting afterwards. That is why upsizing one tier above the minimum is usually money well spent, while jumping two tiers for a small low-risk home mostly buys peace of mind.

How to size for your home: four questions

Start with lightning exposure. Check whether your area sees frequent thunderstorms — the National Weather Service and Siemens' own surge-exposure map both rank the Southeast and Gulf Coast highest. If your neighbourhood loses power in storms several times a year, or neighbours have lost appliances to surges, treat 100 kA as your floor and 140 kA as the sensible pick. If storms are rare and your utility supply is stable, 80 kA is a defensible choice.

Next, count what a surge would actually destroy. A home with a gas furnace board, a refrigerator board, a washer and dryer, a home theatre, a network rack, and a heat-pump controller has thousands of dollars of surge-sensitive electronics, and each board replacement runs $300–$1,200. The more boards, the stronger the case for 108 kA or above. A home with mostly dumb appliances and a couple of TVs can reasonably stop at 80 kA. Also consider your service size: the SPD's rating is independent of your panel's amperage — a 140 kA unit works on a 100-amp panel and a 400-amp panel alike — but a 400-amp home usually has more circuits and more gear behind it, which pushes the sensible tier upward.

Third, look at your panel and wiring path. The SPD should mount as close to the main breaker as possible, with short, straight leads, because every extra inch of lead wire adds impedance that lets more surge voltage through to your circuits. A high-kA device mounted far from the panel on long coiled leads can protect worse than a smaller unit mounted tight to the bus bars. If your panel is crowded with no spare breaker spaces, tell your electrician before buying: some compact breaker-style units, like the FS140, solve a space problem that a larger external enclosure cannot.

Finally, decide whether you need Type 1 as well. A Type 2 panel device handles surges that reach your panel; a Type 1 device at the service entrance intercepts external spikes before they enter the home. In high-lightning areas the layered answer — Type 1 plus Type 2 — is what electricians recommend, and the combined project runs roughly $300–$800 installed. Sizing the Type 2 unit does not change if you add Type 1; the two layers share the work, which extends both devices' lives.

kA is not the only number: VPR, In, and MCOV

A buyer who shops on kA alone misses the number that decides what voltage actually reaches your TV: the voltage protection rating (VPR). VPR is the maximum voltage the SPD lets through during a standard test surge, measured under UL 1449. Lower is better. Most quality residential units clamp at 600 volts line-to-neutral — the FS140, the Eaton CHSPT2ULTRA, and the HEPD80 all share that 600 V figure — while some budget units allow 700 volts or more. A 140 kA device with a 700 V VPR can let more voltage through than an 80 kA device with a 600 V VPR, so compare VPR before you compare kA.

The nominal discharge current (In) is the second supporting number: the surge current the device is rated to survive 15 times without degradation, typically 10 kA or 20 kA for residential units (the HEPD80 is rated 10 kA; the Eaton CHSPT2ULTRA 20 kA). A higher In means the device tolerates repeated medium surges — exactly the utility-switching and restoration spikes that happen most often — without wearing out early. The maximum continuous operating voltage (MCOV) is the highest steady voltage the device tolerates without conducting: 150 volts line-to-neutral and 300 volts line-to-line on a standard 120/240 V home, which gives comfortable headroom over the nominal 120 V and 240 V supply.

Short-circuit current rating (SCCR) matters to your electrician, not to your shopping list: it is the fault current the SPD can safely withstand, typically 22–25 kA for these units, and it must suit your panel's available fault current. The practical takeaway is simple: shortlist by kA tier for your exposure, then check that the VPR is 600 V or lower, the In is at least 10 kA, and the unit is UL 1449 listed. Any device missing a UL 1449 listing, whatever its claimed kA, is not a candidate.

Sizing mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is buying the biggest number and stopping there. A 140 kA unit with no point-of-use protection still leaves your home theatre and office exposed to surges generated inside the house — a large motor or compressor switching off creates a spike on its own circuit that the panel device only partly attenuates. Layering is the fix: a Type 2 panel unit plus $25–$60 plug-in Type 3 units at the expensive gear, which is the same layered strategy commercial buildings use. The panel device handles the big external events; the point-of-use units catch what remains.

The second mistake is treating kA as a guarantee against lightning. No SPD stops a direct lightning strike, at any rating — a direct strike delivers tens of thousands of amps in a fraction of a second, far beyond any residential device. What a properly sized SPD does is handle nearby strikes, utility switching, and restoration surges, which are the events that actually destroy most electronics. Homes in extreme lightning zones should pair their SPD with a dedicated lightning protection system rather than buying a bigger SPD and assuming the job is done.

The third mistake is a DIY install to save the electrician's fee. Sizing the device is the easy part; installing it means working inside the breaker panel, where the service conductors stay live even with the main breaker switched off. If you are unsure about any part of panel work, hire a licensed electrician — the install typically takes one to two hours and $150–$400 all-in for a Type 2 unit. And whatever you buy, check the indicator LEDs after every major storm: a dark LED means the unit has sacrificed itself and is no longer protecting anything, even though your power still works. Replace it immediately, and budget for a replacement every 5 to 10 years as the MOVs wear.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size surge protector do I need for my house?

Most homes need an 80,000 to 140,000 amp (80–140 kA) per-phase Type 2 SPD. Choose 80 kA for a typical suburban home, 100–108 kA for a larger home or one with a home office and theatre, and 140 kA for storm-prone regions or homes with high-value electronics. Anything below about 50 kA is undersized for whole-house duty. Match the tier to your lightning exposure and the value of the electronics behind the panel, not to your panel's amperage.

Is a higher kA rating always better?

Up to a point, yes — more headroom means the device survives bigger events and lasts longer under repeated medium surges. But the gains flatten: a 140 kA unit does not protect better against everyday small surges than an 80 kA unit, because both clamp to about the same voltage (usually 600 V). Above your home's sensible tier you are mostly buying peace of mind, and money spent on a second layer — Type 3 plug-in units at expensive gear — protects better than money spent on a still-bigger panel device.

What does 100kA mean on a surge protector?

It means the device is rated to divert up to 100,000 amps of surge current per phase in a single event, tested under UL 1449. It is a single-event capacity, not a lifetime total and not a measure of everyday protection. Compare per-phase figures only: some listings add modes together for a bigger headline number, which makes an 80 kA device look like a 160 kA one. Check the spec sheet for the per-phase surge current capacity.

Do I need a different surge protector size for a 200-amp panel?

No. The kA rating is independent of your panel's amperage — the same 80 kA, 108 kA, or 140 kA Type 2 unit works on 100-amp, 200-amp, and 400-amp services (the Siemens FS140 is rated for panels up to 400 amps). Panel size matters for mounting space and breaker availability, not for kA sizing. Size by lightning exposure and electronics value instead, and confirm with your electrician that the unit fits your panel.

Is 80kA enough for a whole-house surge protector?

For a typical suburban home with standard electronics and moderate storm exposure, yes — 80 kA is the mainstream recommendation and the tier where devices like the Square D HEPD80 offer strong value around $70. Step up to 108 kA or 140 kA if you live in a high-lightning region such as Florida or the Gulf Coast, run a home office or theatre, or have expensive HVAC and appliance control boards to protect. Whatever tier you choose, verify the VPR is 600 V or lower and the unit is UL 1449 listed.
